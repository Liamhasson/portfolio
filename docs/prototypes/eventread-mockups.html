03 · Problem1 screen · 1440 × ~990
900

Problem

Two bands, one lost night. My band's release show lost its audience to a Måneskin arena show on the same night. A friend's band lost theirs to the Champions League final. Nobody had checked, because there was nothing to check with.

Eventread is built for the two people I worked with for ten years: artists and managers picking a date, and promoters and agents filling a room. Both pick a date and hope: ticketing platforms show their own events, and nobody shows the whole night.

Live Nation alone puts on 54,000 shows a year, about 150 every night. Every independent show gets booked around that calendar, usually without seeing it. Prism.fm, TourSmart and ChatGPT each answer part of the question; none checks a date against everything happening that night.

Solution

The Danger Score. Every competing event gets three weights: how big its room is next to yours, how close its genre is to yours, and how close its start time is to yours. Multiplied together, they place the night in one of three bands: Safe, Threat or Critical.

How it works

One search, two APIs. Ticketmaster covers the big ticketed shows. JamBase catches the smaller, local ones Ticketmaster doesn't list. For Berlin in November 2026 that's 487 shows against Ticketmaster's 93, and 436 of them aren't on Ticketmaster at all. One search checks both, and followed dates send an update when something new lands on your night.

Two fixes behind the search: "Cambridge" returned 124 events across the UK and the US until places carried their country (21 after the fix), and tour shows wanting the same night are assigned in two passes.

06 · Challenges — shared1 screen · 1440 × ~1000
900

Challenges

01

A blank calendar looked safe

Not checkedNo coverage hereAll Saints' Day
What happened

A month with no data looked exactly like a month with no competition. London showed 7 days of events, Tel Aviv came back as a green “No conflicts”, and holidays were missing.

What I did

I gave missing data its own states (“Not checked”, “No coverage here”), showed holidays without scoring them, and added an “announced” indicator built on an 8-city count: about 95% of a month's shows are listed one month out, 1% a year out.

What it changed

London went from 7 visible days to all 31.

02

The score claimed precision it didn't have

SafeThreatCritical
What happened

“Safe 18” on a tour date read like a date or a time, and a 0–100 number promised more precision than the formula has.

What I did

I dropped the number and kept three bands, Safe, Threat and Critical, after checking the cut-points on 3,172 real listings.

What it changed

The formula produced only 27 distinct values, and none fell between 35–45 or 64–80: the bands sit on real gaps.

03

One product, two visual languages

Follow this dateThreatConflicts9
What happened

The interface had grown by feature: pill-shaped navigation next to square buttons, colour used for decoration as much as for risk.

What I did

I surveyed product design systems and built one from three references: Vercel's type, Ramp's colour discipline, Cal.com's month view. Risk became the only colour, shown as a dot and a word.

What it changed

One type family, one set of shapes, three colours that always mean risk, and a CI test that fails if an old style comes back.

How it stacks at 390

  1. Shell stays: the 44px back circle left, the site's three round pills right (13px on phones). No hover on touch: the light shows on keyboard focus, press scales to 0.97.
  2. Hero keeps its order: label, title (48px), line, Visit site. The month view is cropped to the day tiles instead of shrinking the whole app to an unreadable thumbnail.
  3. The navy band runs edge to edge. “Your show” sits above the rows; each row keeps its three factors side by side and moves the formula under them, so the band badge stays next to the event name.
  4. No horizontal scrolling anywhere: genre filters wrap, the formula row is full width.
